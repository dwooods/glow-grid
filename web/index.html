Panel settings

What these settings mean
  • LED brightness: how bright the LEDs run, from 0.05 to 1. The Pi caps it at MAX_BRIGHTNESS (0.4 by default, about 6 A for full white), so anything higher plays at 0.4.
  • Gamma: corrects for how eyes see light. LEDs get brighter in even steps, but we notice dark shades more than bright ones, so without correction mid-tones look washed out. Gamma darkens the middle of the range to compensate. 2.2 is the usual value. Raise it for more contrast and deeper colors (very dark shades can drop to off), lower it toward 1 for a flatter, brighter look.
  • PSU amps: your power supply's rating. It only sets the limit on the Peak meter under the grid.
  • Cols and Rows: the panel's width and height in LEDs. The Pi program is built for 16×16. Saved files record the grid size, and the Pi menu flags a file made for a different size instead of squashing it silently. Making the grid smaller crops every frame; Undo reverts.
  • Saved PNGs remember brightness and gamma, and play.py on the Pi uses them for that image, so the panel matches what you see here.
  • The Panel check uses the same rounding and "keep non-black pixels lit" rule as the Pi, so its warnings match what the panel will do. The grid itself shows your colors as drawn, not as the LEDs will glow.

Barely lit means the pixel isn't black but ends up at 1–3 out of 255 on the LED, so it reads as off or a faint smudge. Use pure black for "off", or brighten it.

Help: how images turn out on the panel

Glow Grid is for drawing pixel art and checking what survives the Pi's brightness cap and gamma curve (the dashed outlines and the Panel check) before it goes near the panel. The Pi fits any image to the grid and plays it. How good it looks depends on what you give it.

Works well

  • Pixel art at or near grid size (drawn here, in Piskel, or the examples) comes out pixel-perfect.
  • Photos and large images: anything more than 2× the grid is averaged per cell, so photos come out smooth instead of noisy.
  • Animations: an animated GIF, or a sprite sheet (frames side by side, as tall as the grid) named with the speed, walk_8fps.png, or _sheet.
  • Transparency becomes "off" LEDs.
  • Non-square images are centered and fitted without stretching.

Animation files: sheet, fps and names

  • Sprite sheet = one PNG with all frames side by side, like a film strip. 4 frames on a 16×16 grid make a 64×16 image. fps = how fast the frames play (4 fps = a quarter second per frame). The sheet says what the file is; fps says how fast to play it.
  • Saved from here: the frame count and fps are stored inside the PNG, so you can rename the file to anything (ghost.png). Brightness and gamma are stored there too, and the Pi menu shows all of them next to the file name.
  • From other tools (Piskel, hand-made strips): the name decides. walk_4fps.png plays at 4 fps (it marks a sheet and sets the speed). walk_sheet.png plays at the default 6 fps. A plain walk.png plays as one squashed still.
  • The Pi's speed order: the number you type, then the fps in the PNG, then _Nfps in the name, then 6.
  • Can lose the stored data: re-saving in another editor, and scaled exports (_x8, which play as stills). Copying with scp keeps it. Avoid spaces in names.

Animating one part: move and still part

  • Move: the arrows in Move and layers (or Shift+arrow keys) shift this frame one pixel. Duplicate a frame, nudge it, repeat: that makes a bob or a slide.
  • Animate one part only: draw the whole character and duplicate it for each frame, then tick the box. Everything identical in all frames becomes the still part, leaving each frame with only what changes (hand, legs, eyes, mouth). Paint just those bits per frame. Black on a frame is see-through. Untick to join it back.
  • Saved files always combine the layers, so the Pi and the sprite sheet need nothing new.

Drawing tools

  • Undo and redo: Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Y, or the buttons. Clear frame, Delete frame and Start over can all be undone.
  • Shade (the slider beside the color wheel) sets how light or dark the paint color is, from black at 0 to the full color at 100. It only changes the color you paint with. It is not the LED brightness in Panel settings, which sets how bright the panel runs.
  • Erase sets a pixel to black, which is "off" on the panel. Pick (or Alt+click while painting) takes the color under the cursor and goes back to Paint.
  • Panel check (under Panel settings) shows the estimated current draw and how many pixels are barely lit or will shift color. Its one-line summary shows a warning color when anything is flagged.

Drawing on a phone

  • The grid fills the width. At the bottom is a dock that stays put: Paint, Fill, Erase and Pick, your color (tap it to jump to the color wheel), the palette (swipe it sideways) and Undo and Redo. Under the grid is a strip of frames with Play, + to duplicate a frame and More.
  • Swipe up (anywhere except on the grid, where a finger paints) to scroll the rest of the controls into view: frame speed, blank, delete and start over, Move and layers, the color wheel and hex, Clear frame and the Panel settings. More jumps straight to them. Turn the phone sideways and the page goes back to the plain scrolling layout.
  • The magnifier: while a finger or pen is painting or erasing, a close-up of the pixels around it floats beside your finger, with the pixel you are on outlined. It appears only for touch and pen, never for a mouse.

Getting a picture onto the panel

File ▸ Import image loads everything: a sprite sheet or single frame saved from here comes back as separate frames; any other picture opens a crop-and-zoom editor. Tick Exact pixels for pixel art, or leave it off for photos. Both can remove the background. On a phone the picker offers the camera too.

  • Save for the Pi makes one PNG with one pixel per LED. One frame saves a still, two or more save a sprite sheet. Brightness, gamma, grid size and (for an animation) frame count and fps go inside the file, so the name can stay plain.
  • Copy it to the Pi from your PC, then run glow-grid, press r to rescan and pick the file. In PowerShell: scp "$HOME\Downloads\sprite.png" user@raspberrypi.local:~/glow-grid/images/ (change the file name and login to match yours).
  • View or share (PNG) saves a sharp, enlarged copy (named _x16 etc.) to look at or send to someone. Don't copy it to the Pi: an enlarged animation would play as one squashed still.
  • ESP32-C3 export comes with the C3 build.

Watch out for

  1. Mid-size pixel art (between 1× and 2× the grid, e.g. a 24×24 sprite) keeps exact pixels, so some detail is dropped. Draw at grid size, or at an exact 2×, 3×… upscale.
  2. Sprite sheets from other tools play as one squashed still unless the name has _8fps or _sheet. Sheets saved from here carry their frame count and speed inside the PNG, so renaming them is safe.
  3. Wiring: until the probe settings are set and confirmed on the Pi (glow-grid probe, glow-grid probe check, then k in the menu), images may look scrambled; the menu warns you.
  4. Near-black and dark colors: near-black isn't off, and dark shades can vanish. The Panel check outlines both.

Export

One frame saves a still; two or more save a sprite sheet. Brightness, gamma, frame count and speed are stored inside the PNG.

Import image

Drag the photo to move it. Pinch or scroll to zoom. The faint lines are one LED each; the small picture is the result.

Only background connected to the picture's edge is removed, so white inside the subject (a face, an eye) stays. If the background leaks inside through a gap in an outline, lower the tolerance.

Flat art finds the picture's main colors and gives each LED whichever one covers most of its cell, so outlines and soft edges disappear and the result is already in a small palette. Use it for illustrations, logos and clip art; use averaging or dithering for photos. Dithering is baked in at the current brightness and gamma.
